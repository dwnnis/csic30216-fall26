Week 1 Self-Paced Module: History of Language Models

{{ site.course_code }} · {{ site.course_semester }}

Overview

In Monday's lecture, you heard about the engineer's new role in the agentic era: specifying intent, evaluating trajectories, and knowing when to take control. You would probably ask yourself: “where did these tools come from?” This is what this week's self-paced module is for. You don't need to understand the math — you will just need to get what the intuition is. By the end of the module, you'll know why these models work the way they do, which will make you a better prompter, evaluator, and engineer working with AI.

Why This Matters

This helps you, very briefly, understand how LLMs work, and how that shapes how you use them. If you know a model is predicting the next token based on probability distributions, you'll prompt differently than if you think it is just retrieving facts from a database. If you understand what embeddings do, you'll make better decisions about which tools to build. If you know the difference between pre-training and instruction tuning, you'll understand why models behave differently and what safety training actually costs.

Optional Materials

If you want to go deeper on any of these topics (not required, but helpful if you're curious):

  • Jay Alammar, “The Illustrated Word2Vec,” 2019 — a great, illustrated article on word embeddings and Word2Vec, helpful if you want to go deeper into the topic.
  • Anthropic, “Core Views on AI Safety,” 2023 — the least “technical” of this batch, but provides a great overview of alignment and why it is an important issue in the landscape of rapid model advancement today.
  • Simon Willison, “Catching up on the weird world of LLMs,” 2023 — slightly outdated, but a good starting point to dive deeper into LLM capabilities and the logic behind them, including a brief history of LLM advancement up to when we were first startled by what it could do.
