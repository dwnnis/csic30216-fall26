Week 3 Self-Paced Module: How Engineering Teams Work with AI

{{ site.course_code }} · {{ site.course_semester }}

Overview

Today's lab asked you to write your team's AI usage guidelines and an evaluation plan under time pressure — which means what you produced is exactly that: a first draft. This module gives you the real-world evidence to calibrate against: how actual engineering teams write guidelines like these, run evaluations, and handle the AI-specific risks of a shipped system. By the end, you should be able to reread your own team's draft and tell whether it would survive contact with a new teammate joining in Week 10 — or with a customer who took its output at face value.

Why This Matters

DORA's 2025 State of AI-assisted Software Development report — this week's required reading — found that AI functions as an amplifier: it strengthens high-performing teams' existing practices and magnifies the dysfunctions of struggling ones. Teams with a “clear and communicated AI stance” see that amplification lean positive, with AI's benefits on individual effectiveness and organizational performance amplified, and friction reduced — compared to teams that leave AI use to individual discretion. This is the empirical case for the exercise today's lab put you through, and the report's two named case studies — Adidas and Booking.com — show what that looks like at the scale of 1,000+ and 3,000+ developers respectively.

What You'll Learn

  • Evaluation approaches in depth — what structured observation, pre/post surveys, and brief interviews actually look like when run within a semester project, and why you need at least one of them with people outside your team.
  • How real teams adopt AI tooling — DORA's finding that a clear, communicated AI stance amplifies AI's benefits and reduces friction, and why the specific form of a team's norm matters less than the fact of having one at all.
  • What the case studies show — Adidas saw 20–30% productivity gains from AI in loosely coupled architecture, but little benefit in teams tightly coupled to legacy systems; Booking.com's adoption stayed uneven until they invested in training developers to give AI more explicit instructions and context, after which merge requests rose by up to 30%. Neither story is really about the AI tool itself — one is about architecture, the other about training.
  • Disclosure and liability — the Air Canada chatbot case, where a court held the company responsible for incorrect information its own system gave a customer. “The AI said it” is not a defence, and documenting your design decisions is your primary protection.
